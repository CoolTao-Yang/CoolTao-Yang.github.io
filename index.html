Research

A working thesis: environments shape learning more than algorithms do. Define a useful environment, choose a cheap-but-confident reward, and control the uncertainty band you live with — the policy follows.

At the tactical level I work on a recurring theme I call "more information for enhanced attention" — injecting structured priors (spatial geometry, graph topology, evaluation feedback) as multiplicative bias on attention and training pathways, so that weak signals become visible and learnable. The priors I use change with the problem family; the principle does not.

Specific topics:

  1. Environment design for evaluation & training
    Treating evaluation signal as the auxiliary information that defines a useful learning environment. How do we turn failure cases, capability profiles, and bench-derived rubrics into training-time gradient signal? Forthcoming at Humanlaya AI (Jun 2026).
  2. Population dynamics & Mean Field Games
    A long-standing mathematical interest. I worry that pure ML approaches under-use the language of coupled HJB / Fokker-Planck systems for populations of agents under shared dynamics — both in evaluation (where agent behaviour is necessarily collective) and in eval-train co-design.
  3. RL: define the environment, not the policy
    I'm sceptical of architecture-tinkering RL. Most leverage comes from defining the right environment, a cheap-but-confident reward, and a tractable uncertainty band — measured via AB tests, KL divergence, and statistical hypothesis testing. Less is more; from large to small.
  4. Structured priors for attention
    Injecting Manhattan-distance decay, graph topology, or evaluation feedback as multiplicative bias on the attention kernel. Cuts complexity (O(L²) → O(L)) while preserving the problem's physics or topology. Representative work: PhysEDA (NeurIPS 2026 submission, under review).
